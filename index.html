6.2 Overview of Photosynthesis

Interactive Guide

Photosynthesis feeds most life on Earth. Sunlight is abundant, but it cannot directly power metabolic reactions. First, it must be converted into the chemical bond energy of organic molecules.


Click on any structure inside the Chloroplast diagram on the left to see how the two stages of photosynthesis work together!

Take-Home Message 6.2:
• Stage 1 (Light-Dependent): Occurs at the thylakoid membrane. Converts light energy to chemical energy (ATP & NADPH) and splits water to release O2.
• Stage 2 (Light-Independent): Occurs in the stroma. Uses ATP and NADPH to assemble sugars from CO2 and water.

Why Blue & Red Work Best: Chlorophyll a absorbs violet, blue, orange, and red light strongly, while reflecting green light (500–580 nm). Accessory pigments like chlorophyll b and carotenoids broaden the range of wavelengths that can drive photosynthesis.
Key Concepts (Section 6.3):
• Photons & Wavelength: Shorter wavelengths (violet/blue, ~400 nm) carry more energy per photon than longer wavelengths (red, ~700 nm).
• Light-Trapping Region: Pigments have alternating single and double carbon bonds where electrons absorb photons with the exact energy needed to jump to a higher shell.

Why Two Pathways? (Section 6.4):
The noncyclic pathway produces both ATP and NADPH using electrons pulled from water. However, it doesn't yield quite enough ATP to balance NADPH use in the Calvin–Benson cycle—so the cyclic pathway runs to supply extra ATP and protect cells under intense light!

Hot, Dry Day (Stomata Closed):
C3 Plants (e.g., Barley, Bluegrass) & Photorespiration:
When stomata close on hot, dry days to conserve water, O₂ builds up and CO₂ drops. Rubisco attaches O₂ instead of CO₂ to RuBP (photorespiration), losing fixed carbon and wasting ATP across the chloroplast, peroxisome, and mitochondrion (Fig. 6.15).
